A summary helps a reader connect your experience to a role. Some employers read it closely. Others move straight to experience or projects. Keep it brief, specific, and supported by the rest of your resume.

By the end of this module, you will have one summary for a target posting and a check for unsupported claims.

______

## Build from Evidence

Read the posting, then choose three items from your resume:

1. Your current field or target role.
2. One relevant strength with a concrete example.
3. A result, deliverable, or project that supports the strength.

A short pattern is: `Role and context. Relevant skill and where you used it. Verified outcome or deliverable.` Use fewer sentences when your evidence is stronger without an introduction.

> Backend engineer with experience maintaining data ingestion services. Built Python checks for failed imports and documented the recovery procedure. Reduced manual triage from 40 to 15 minutes per batch, based on team logs.

Those details are illustrative. Replace them with your own verified work. If you have no measurement, name the delivered change without inventing a number.

For a career transition, name the connection between past work and the target role:

> Operations coordinator moving into IT support. Documented device setup for a 35-person team and resolved recurring access requests with the help desk. Built a home lab to practice account and network troubleshooting.

______

## Revise for the Role

Compare your draft with the posting. Use its vocabulary where the term accurately describes your experience. A local keyword tool might highlight an omitted term, but no general rule gives the summary a fixed rank or weight across hiring systems.

Remove claims such as `results-driven` when the resume offers no supporting result. Remove an objective that only states you want the job. A new graduate or career changer might use one line to state a target role when the connection is otherwise unclear.

Avoid repeating the job title without evidence. `Software engineer with software development experience` tells the reader little. A project, tool, scope, or outcome gives the reader something to assess.

______

## The Copy-Paste Check

1. Hide your name and employer names.
2. Ask another reader to identify the work or project described in your summary.
3. Point to the corresponding bullet or project in the resume for each claim.
4. Delete or rewrite any sentence that fits many unrelated candidates or lacks evidence.

Write the summary after you choose your strongest bullets. Update it for a different role only when your relevant evidence changes. A summary is optional when the first experience or project entry already makes your fit clear.
